\section{Principles of Quantum Physics}
In this section, we will introduce the principles in an axiomatic form. We will formulate them in algebraic manner appropriate for quantum information and computation in finite dimensional Hilbert space.
\begin{parag}{Hilbert space}
Note here that we have not defined what a Hilbert space here is so let me define it (wikipedia)
\begin{definition}{(Hilbert Space)}
A \important{Hilbert} space is a real or complex inner product space that is also a complete metric space with respect to the distance function induced by the inner product
\end{definition}
To say that a complex vector space $\mathcal{H}$ is a complex inner product space means that there is an inner product $\langle x, y\rangle$ associating a complex number to each pair of elements $x, y$ of $\mathcal{H}$ that satisfies the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The inner product is conjugate symmetric; that is the inner product of a pair of elements is equal to the complex conjugate of the inner product of the swapped elements:
	\begin{align*} 
		\langle y, x\rangle = \overline{\langle x, y\rangle} 
	\end{align*}
	Importantly, this implies that $\langlex, x\rangle$ is a real number
	\item The inner product is linear in its first argument. For all complex number $a, b \in\mathbb{C}$
		\begin{align*} 
			\langle ax_1 + bx_2, y\rangle  = a\langle x_1, y\rangle + b\langle x_2, y\rangle
		\end{align*}
	\item The inner product of an element with itself is positive definite 
		\begin{align*} 
			\langle x, x\rangle &> 0 \mathspace \mathspace \text{ if } x \neq 0\\
			 \langle x, x\rangle &=  0 \mathspace \mathspace \text{ if } x = 0
		\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}


\end{parag}

\subsection{Principle 1: State vectors}

The state of a physical system can be described (isolated from the rest of the universe) as a vector in a finite dimensional Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$, we denote it as
\begin{align*} 
	\ket{\psi} \in \mathcal{H} 
\end{align*}
This vector $\ket{\psi}$ must be normalized with respect to the inner product of that Hilbert space, meaning $\braket{\psi}{\psi} = 1$.
\begin{framedremark}
The principle doesn't say how to choose $\ket{\psi}$ neither the space itself $\mathcal{H}$. This decision comes from the underlying physics of the system.
\end{framedremark}
\begin{framedremark}
In a non-isolated system we will have to generalize this principle. This will be done in later part of the course when introducing the notion of \important{density} matrix.
\end{framedremark}
    Hence let us start with the most basic example in quantum physics, the qbit! The Hilbert space describing a qbit is given as $\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2$ which can be denoted as 
	\begin{align*} 
		\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2 &= \left\{\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}, \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C} \right\} \\
								   &= \left\{\alpha \ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}, \alpha \in \mathbb{C}, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\right\}
	\end{align*}
	Furthermore for this state to be correctly the state of a qbit it also must satisfy normalization, meaning its norm is 1. Recall not the definition of the inner product of a Hilbert space did we even define our inner product here? Recall that we need to validate the three properties above, a valid choice here is the following inner product:
	\begin{align*} 
		\langle x, y\rangle  = \sum_{i= 1}^{n} x_i^{\ast}y_i
	\end{align*}
	You can check that this inner product here fulfils all properties. Going back on our requirement ($\left\|x\right\| = 1$) we will therefore need
	\begin{align*} 
		\alpha^\ast\alpha + \beta^\ast\beta = 1
	\end{align*}
	With that requirement, we have the Hilbert space of a qbit.\\
	$ $\\
	Also in terms of notation, we will often use the letter $\psi$, $\varphi$ and $ \phi$ in order to represent quantum states. We will also use the term state to refer to quantum state as all work here is primarily done in the quantum world. Hence we define our qbit as 
	\begin{align*} 
		\ket{\psi} = \alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1} 
	\end{align*}
	For more general states, ones of dimension $d$ (i.e. $\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^{d}$) our state is constructed as 
	\begin{align*} 
		\ket{\psi} = \sum_{i= 1}^{d} \alpha_i \ket{i}
	\end{align*}
	\begin{framedremark}
	    Note that state doesn't have to be necessarily finite. A good example of this are quantum harmonic oscillators (so PHYS-344) in which our state is defined as 
		\begin{align*} 
			 \ket{\Psi} = \sum_{n= 0}^{\infty} \psi_n \ket{n}
		\end{align*}
	\end{framedremark}



\subsection{Principle 2: Unitary evolution on time evolution}
The state of an isolated system evolves (as a function of time) unitarily. This means that of $\ket{\psi_0}$ is a state at time $= 0$, the state $\ket{\psi_t}$ is given by 
\begin{align*} 
	U_t \ket{\psi_0}  = \ket{\psi_t}
\end{align*}
Where $U_t^{\dagger}U_t = U_tU_t^{\dagger} = I$ i.e. $U_t$ is unitary (usually time dependent) matrix
\begin{framedremark}
One can show that unitary matrix represent rotation of the space by a specified angles.
\end{framedremark}

\begin{framedremark}
	For people of have done or who are doing MATH-310 (Algebra), one can see that the time evolution matrices form a group in the sense that 
	\begin{align*} 
		U_{t_2}U_{t_1} U_{t_1 + t_2} \mathspace \mathspace , U_{t= 0} = I
	\end{align*}
\end{framedremark}


\begin{parag}{Example: perfectly reflecting mirror}
    Let us take the example of a reflecting mirror :
	\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figure/2026-10-19.pdf}
	\end{center}
	The physical process which transforms the incident ray into the reflected ray is described by the unitary matrix:
	\begin{align*} 
		X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}  = \ket{H}\bra{V} + \ket{V}\bra{H}
	\end{align*}
	For $\ket{H}= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} $ and $\ket{V} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} $. Let us take a step back on what is actually going on here. What a perfectly reflecting mirror with a tilted angle here reflect a ray that is normal  ($\braket{\psi_{t_1}}{\psi_{t_2}} = 0$) for the state after and before the mirror, so here we would require that $U\ket{H} = \ket{V}$ and vis versa which if you apply $X$ on any of those two states gives the intended results.
\end{parag}
\begin{parag}{Semi-transparent mirror}
    Now let us define the same mirror but in this case we want '\textit{half}' of the rays to reflect off the mirror and the other half to go through it. Given the initial state $\ket{H}$ the output that we are looking for is $\frac{\ket{H} + \ket{V}}{\sqrt{2}}$. On the other hand given a vector $\ket{V}$ we want the final state $\frac{\ket{H} - \ket{V}}{\sqrt{2}}$
	\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figure/2026-10-20.pdf}
	\end{center}
	The unitary matrix representing this operation is called the Hadamard matrix (gate) defined as 
	\begin{align*} 
		H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}  
	\end{align*}
	\begin{framedremark}
	One can find it using only the two conditions asked above. We require that $H\ket{H} = \frac{\ket{H} + \ket{V}}{\sqrt{2}}$ which implies that 
	\begin{align*} 
		H\ket{H} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{H} + \ket{V}\right)\bra{H}
	\end{align*}
	We also require tat $H\ket{V} = \frac{\ket{H} - \ket{V}}{\sqrt{2}}$ giving 
	\begin{align*} 
		H\ket{V} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\left(\ket{H} - \ket{V}\right)\bra{\ket{V}}} 
	\end{align*}
	hence our matrix $H$ is given by 
	\begin{align*} 
		H &=  \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left(\ket{H} + \ket{V}\right)\bra{H} + \left(\ket{H} - \ket{V}\right)\bra{V}\right) \\&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{H}\bra{H} + \ket{V}\bra{H} + \ket{H}\bra{V} - \ket{V}\bra{V}\right)\\
		&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} 
	\end{align*}
	\end{framedremark}
\end{parag}

\subsection{Principle 3: Observable quantities}
\begin{center}
An observable (or measurable) quantity (such as energy, position, momentum, magnetic moment, polarization) is given by an Hermitian matrix $A$ (i.e. $A^{\dagger} = A$)
\end{center}

\begin{parag}{Example}
    Let us take for instance the example of a simple qbit in the Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$. It can be easily shown that any hermitian matrix are of the form 
	\begin{align*} 
		A &= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \overline{\beta} & \gamma \end{pmatrix}  \\
								   &= \alpha \ket{0}\bra{0} + \beta \ket{0}\bra{1} + \overline{\beta}\ket{1}\bra{0} + \gamma \ket{1}\bra{1}
	\end{align*}
	Such $2 \times 2$ matrices can be represented as linear combination of so-called Pauli matrices 
	\begin{align*} 
		I &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}  \\
		\sigma_x &= X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\
		\sigma_y &= Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\
		\sigma_z &= Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} 
	\end{align*}
	\begin{framedremark}
		Pauli matrices have a lot of useful properties and are used in everywhere in quantum physics (\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_matrices}{wiki}), there are very useful as they represent projection of a qbit on the axis $x, y, z$ when the qbit is represented on the Bloch sphere. Meaning that given the measurement of the qbit using this basis, one can deduce the projection of the qbit on those axis (on the Bloch sphere).
	\end{framedremark}
	\begin{subparag}{Exercises}
	    One can show that any $2 \times 2$ matrices can be represented as a linear combination of those matrices, that is, for any $A$ s.t. $A =  A^{\dagger}$, $A$ can be written as 
		\begin{align*} 
			A = a_0 I + a_x \sigma_x + a_y \sigma_y + a_z \sigma_z 
		\end{align*}
		With $a_0, a_x, a_y, a_z \in \mathbb{R}$.
	\end{subparag}
\end{parag}
\subsection{Spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices}
Let $A$ be a $d \times d$ Hermitian matrix. Let $\ket{v_1}, \ldots, \ket{v_d}$ and $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d$ the eigenvectors and eigenvalues, i.e.
\begin{align*} 
	A \ket{v_i} = \lambda_i \ket{v_i} , \forall i = 1, \ldots d 
\end{align*}
The eigenvectors can always be chosen to form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^{d}$ and the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d$ are real ($\in \mathbb{R}$). Moreover, on can use the \important{spectral decomposition} giving 
\begin{align*}
	A = \sum_{i= 1}^{d} \lambda_i \bra{v_i}\ket{v_i} 
\end{align*}
\begin{framedremark}
The last formula just says that in the basis $\ket{v_1}, \ldots, \ket{v_d}$ of eigenvectors, $A$ is diagonal with each $\lambda_i$ at the $A_{ii}$ positions.\\
$ $\\
Remark also that this definition is a lot useful here as it can be used to define functions applied to matrices. Let us for instance wanting to exponentiate our matrix $A$, meaning that we have a function $f$ (the exponential) and seek to find $f\left(A\right)$. One can define (with functional analysis) 
\begin{align*} 
	\exp\left(A\right) = \sum_{i= 1}^{d}\exp\left(\lambda_i\right) \ket{v_i}\bra{v_i} 
\end{align*}
This exponentiation here is pretty useful as one can therefore solve differential equation involving matrices (e.g. Schrödinger's equation).
\end{framedremark}

\subsection{Principle 4: Measurement postulate}
\begin{center}
    let $\ket{\psi}$ be a state of a system. Let $A$ be an observable that we measure.\\
	The state of the measurement is a random outcome where the state becomes 
	\begin{align*} 
		\ket{v_i} \mathspace \mathspace \forall i = 1, \ldots d 
	\end{align*}
	The '\textit{value of $A$}' is $\lambda_i$ (associated to $\ket{v_i}$)\\
	$ $\\
	And the probability law is 
	\begin{align*} 
		P\left(i\right) = \left|\braket{v_i}{\psi}\right|^2 
	\end{align*}
	Given by the squared bracket between $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{v_i}$.
\end{center}
\begin{parag}{Second form of the measurement postulate often used in information theory}
    \begin{center}
		A measurement process is performed thanks to a 'measurement apparatus'. The measurement apparatus is modelled by an orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space $\left\{\ket{v_1}, \ket{v_2}, \ldots, \ket{v_d}\right\}$ and the measurement outcome is a random state $\ket{v_i}$ with $P\left(i\right) = \left|\braket{v_i}{\psi}\right|^2$
    \end{center}
	\begin{subparag}{Remark}
	    The second form of the measurement postulate is used when we do not need to specify the observable being measured. Only the '\textit{measurement apparatus}' is specified i.e. an orthonormal basis.
	\end{subparag}
\end{parag}
\begin{parag}{Property}
    The probabilities in the measurement postulate sum to one, here is the proof
	\begin{align*} 
		\sum_{i= 1}^{d} P\left(i\right) &= \sum_{i= 1}^{d} \left|\braket{v_i}{\psi}\right|^2\\
										&= \sum_{i= 1}^{d} \overline{\braket{v_i}{\psi}} \braket{v_i}{\psi}\\
										&= \sum_{i= 1}^{d} \braket{\psi}{v_i}\braket{v_i}{\psi}\\
										&=  \bra{\psi} \left(\sum_{i= 1}^{d}\ket{v_i}\bra{v_i}\right)\ket{\psi}\\
										&= \braket{\psi}{\psi} = 1
	\end{align*}
	Here we have used the fact that $\sum_{i= 1}^{d}\ket{v_i}\bra{v_i} = I$.
\end{parag}

\subsection{Average value of an observable}
Okay we have seen that we have now probability distribution over the eigenvectors of our observable, the question that this is raising is can we find a form of expected value? Well the answer is yes! and we can derive it ourself! For that let us recall the definition of the expected value in the classical case. Given a random variable $X$ we define its expected value as (kind of)
\begin{align*} 
	\mathbb{E}\left[X\right] = \sum_{i= 1}^{n} X_i P(X_i) 
\end{align*}
Here the probability are the defined $\overline{\braket{v_i}{\psi}}\braket{v_i}{\psi}$ and the result of the random variable are the $\lambda_i$ hence our expected value would be defined as 
\begin{align*} 
	\text{Av}\left(A\right) &= \sum_{i= 1}^{d}  \lambda_i \braket{\psi}{v_i} \braket{v_i}{\psi}\\
							&= \bra{\psi}\left(\sum_{i= 1}^{d} \lambda_i\ket{v_i}\bra{v_i}\right)\ket{\psi}\\
							&= \bra{\psi} A \ket{\psi}
\end{align*}
Which is a real value since $\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$ for any Hermitian matrix.

\subsection{Heisenberg uncertainty principle}
Let us define the mean square error (or the square root of the variance) of measurements for two observables $A$ and $B$:
\begin{align*} 
	\Delta A = \sqrt{\bra{\psi}A^2 \ket{\psi} - \bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}^2} \\
	\Delta B = \sqrt{\bra{\psi}B^2 \ket{\psi} - \bra{\psi}B\ket{\psi}^2}
\end{align*}
We have

\begin{align*} 
	\left(\Delta A\right)\left(\Delta B\right) \geq \frac{1}{2}\left| \bra{\psi}\left[A, B\right]\ket{\psi}\right| 
\end{align*}
Where $\left[A, B\right] \equiv AB - BA$ (the commutator)\\
$ $\\
What this property says that the foundations of $A$ and $B$ cannot simultaneously vanish if they do not commute (i.e. $\left[A, B\right] \neq 0$).


\subsection{Principle 5: Composition of systems}
Let $\mathcal{H}_A$ be the Hilbert space of a system $A$ and $\mathcal{H}_B$ be the Hilbert space of a system $B$. Then $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ is the Hilbert space of the composed system $A \bigcup B$. The state of the composed system is  similarly a vector in $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$.
\begin{parag}{Example}
    Let us have the following spaces
	\begin{align*} 
		\mathcal{H}_A = \mathbb{C}^2\\
		\mathcal{H}_B = \mathbb{C}^2
	\end{align*}
	With for instance $\mathcal{H}_A$ being the first qbit (e.g. first photon polarization) and $\mathcal{H}_B$ be the second qbit (e.g. second photon polarization). In that case states are of dimension $4$ and live inside $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2$.
\end{parag}

\subsubsection{Mathematical interlude, Important definition}
Before going down into composition of system, we need first to have notation for our new states, we need  product states and entangled states.
\begin{definition}{(product states)}
	Let $\ket{\psi} \in \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$. It is said to be a \important{product state} if there exists $\ket{\varphi_A} \in \mathcal{H}_A, \ket{\varphi_B} \in \mathcal{H}_B$ such that 
	\begin{align*} 
		\ket{\psi} = \ket{\varphi_A} \otimes \ket{\varphi_B} 
	\end{align*}
\end{definition}


\begin{definition}{(Entangled states)}
A state $\ket{\psi} \in \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ is said to be \important{entangled} if it is not a product state.
\end{definition}

As we will see this classification of states plays a very important role in quantum information processing (e.g. quantum teleportation)

\begin{parag}{Examples}
    \begin{align*} 
    	\ket{0} \otimes \ket{0}, \mathspace \ket{0} \otimes \ket{1} , \ket{1} \otimes \ket{0}, \ldots
    \end{align*}
	Are product states, one can also construct linear combination of such 
	\begin{align*} 
		\left(\frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \otimes \left(\frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0} \otimes \ket{0} + \ket{0} \otimes \ket{1} + \ket{1} \otimes \ket{0} + \ket{1} \otimes \ket{1}\right)
	\end{align*}
	Is a product state. 
	\begin{align*} 
		\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}  \left(\ket{0} \otimes \ket{0} + \ket{1} \otimes \ket{1}\right)
	\end{align*}
	On the other side is entangled (also called Bell state).
\end{parag}


\section{Geometrical representation of qbits and the Bloch sphere}

Quantum state vectors belong to Hilbert space which consist of vectors with complex components. There are \important{note} vectors in usual Euclidean space and thus difficult to represent geometrically.\\
For the particular of \important{one qbit} where is a convenient geomtrical representation which we will often use an given good intuition (for two qbits the situation is already more complicated and for multiple qbits (large) this constitutes an open problem).


Hence, let us work through it for one qbit, recall that its space is $\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2$ 
\begin{align*} 
	\mathcal{H} =  \left\{\alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1}; \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\right\}
\end{align*}
Where $\left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2 = 1$. This implies that we have 4 real parameters with one constraints which implies that we only need 3 parameters to fully describe the space. In fact one should still remove one more parameter. The reason being that is the following :
\begin{center}
	\begin{align*} 
		\ket{\psi}\mathspace \text{ and } \mathspace e^{i \gamma}\ket{\psi}  \mathspace \mathspace, \gamma \in \mathbb{R}
	\end{align*}
	are equivalent physically
\end{center}
Indeed $\gamma$ can't move the observed! To get an intuition about this recall that the probabilities in the measurement postulate satisfy the following 
\begin{align*} 
	\left|\braket{v_i}{\psi}\right|^2
\end{align*}
Now imagine that we where to shift the state by $e^{i \gamma}$ we would have the following probabilities 
\begin{align*} 
	\left|\bra{v_i}e^{i \gamma}\ket{\psi}\right| &= \left|e^{i \gamma}\right|^2 \left|\braket{v_i}{\psi}\right|^2\\
													   &= \left|\braket{v_i}{\psi}\right|^2
\end{align*}

In reality this implies that we should state principle 1 in a more fundamental form stating that 
\begin{center}
    state vector of an isolated system are '\textit{rays}' in the Hilbert space e.g. $e^{i \gamma}\ket{\psi}$, $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$.
\end{center}
\begin{framedremark}
In $e^{ i \gamma}\ket{\psi}$ $\gamma$ is called a \important{global phase}. It \important{cannot be observed} but if you have $\ket{0} + e^{i \gamma}\ket{1}$ for example this is a \important{local phase}. And a local phase \important{can be observed} (e.g. in the interference experiments).
\end{framedremark}

Then for any qbit represented by the state $\ket{\psi} = \alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1}$, we can multiply by a global phase we arrive to make the component $\alpha$ real. What this gives is a grand total of $2$ parameters eventually.
\begin{parag}{The canonical and useful parametrisation}
    So we have a vector of \textit{norm} one and two free parameters. A good idea here would be to represent our state on a sphere based on those two parameters, let us define 
	\begin{align*} 
		\alpha =  \cos \frac{\theta}{2}; \mathspace \mathspace \beta = \left(\sin \frac{\theta}{2}\right)e^{i \phi} 
	\end{align*}
	Giving $\theta \in \left[0, \pi\right]$ and $\varphi \in \left[0, 2\pi\right]$.

\end{parag}
